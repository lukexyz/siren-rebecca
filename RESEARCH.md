## RICHMOND LATTIMORE (1965)

**Full title:** The Odyssey of Homer  
**Publisher:** Harper & Row, 1965 (Harper Perennial Modern Classics paperback still in print)  
**Pages:** ~368  
**Meter/Style:** Loose six-beat accentual line, free verse; echoes Greek dactylic hexameter without strict metre; preserves Homeric repetition and epithets  
**Notes:** Academic default citation for decades; companion volume by Peter V. Jones (Duckworth, 1988) written specifically to accompany it. Still assigned in many classics and comparative literature programmes.

### Reviews

#### Dimock, George — *The Hudson Review*, 1967
- **Verdict:** Strongly positive
- **Review title:** "The Best Yet: Lattimore's Odyssey"
- **Key argument:** Considers it the finest English rendering then available; the review title itself became widely cited
- **Source:** *The Hudson Review*, Vol. 19/20, 1967

#### Times Literary Supplement (year unconfirmed)
- **Verdict:** Very positive
- **Key quote:** "A landmark in the history of modern translation"
- **Source:** Cited in secondary reception scholarship (primary issue not retrieved)

#### New York Times Book Review (year unconfirmed)
- **Verdict:** Very positive
- **Key quote:** "The best translation there is"
- **Source:** Cited in secondary reception scholarship

#### Carne-Ross, D. S. — *Arion*, 1968
- **Affiliation:** Classical scholar and editor of *Arion*, *Delos*, and *Parnassus*; collected essays published as *Classics and Translation* (Bucknell UP, 2010)
- **Verdict:** Savage / negative
- **Essay title:** "A Mistaken Ambition of Exactness: Richmond Lattimore's Odyssey"
- **Key arguments:**
  - Attacks Lattimore's description of Odysseus building the raft as a specimen of mistranslation
  - Criticises removal or flattening of significant epithets
  - Argues the claim of exactness is illusory — a misguided literalism that fails both as poetry and as scholarship: "even if Lattimore's many translations from the Greek have been justly praised, they have not been accurately described"
  - The essay's influence has been cited as a primary driver of decline in Lattimore's academic reputation since the 1970s
- **Source:** *Arion* (1968); reprinted in *Classics and Translation: Essays by D. S. Carne-Ross*, ed. Kenneth Haynes (Bucknell UP, 2010)

#### Jones, Peter V. — *Homer's Odyssey: A Companion* (Duckworth, 1988)
- **Verdict:** Corrective / critical in places
- **Key argument:** Identifies specific lines where Lattimore is inaccurate or misleading; the companion is partly a corrective apparatus on Lattimore's choices
- **Source:** Duckworth, 1988; revised ed. Bristol Classical Press

## ROBERT FITZGERALD (1961)

**Full title:** The Odyssey  
**Publisher:** Doubleday, 1961 (Farrar, Straus and Giroux in print; Scribner deluxe ed. 2025)  
**Meter/Style:** Flexible blank verse; elevated, allusive, influenced by Pound and Keats; described as "Poundian Homer"  
**Awards:** Bollingen Prize in Poetry  
**Notes:** Considered by many scholars the finest *poetic* rendering in English. Described as "the only [Odyssey translation] admired in its own right as a great poem in English." Standard classroom text for three generations.

### Reviews

#### Steiner, George — *Kenyon Review*, Spring 1961 (Vol. 23, No. 2)
- **Affiliation:** Critic, theorist, Cambridge / Geneva; author of *After Babel* (1975)
- **Verdict:** Celebratory
- **Key arguments:**
  - Praises Fitzgerald for solving "the dilemma of adequate language" via "a mode which is at once neutral and modern, lyric yet full of technical resource"
  - "Written in flexible blank verse, Fitzgerald's narrative moves with such ease of tread that we often forget the sheer virtuosity of the artisan"
  - Compares favourably to Chapman and Pope: "Fitzgerald is taking his place beside Chapman and Pope in the unbroken lineage of English Homeric translations"
- **Source:** *Kenyon Review* Vol. 23, No. 2 (Spring 1961)

#### Johnston, Ian — translator, cited in BMCR 2010.02.11
- **Verdict:** Considers Fitzgerald the finest for poetic quality
- **Key argument:** Rates Fitzgerald above Fagles despite some archaising; describes it as the best overall poetic achievement
- **Source:** BMCR 2010.02.11 (Clayton reviewing Johnston's own translation)

#### Clayton, Barbara — BMCR 2010.02.11
- **Key argument:** Cites Fagles, Fitzgerald, and Lombardo as the three translations against which any new translation must be measured; Fitzgerald retains benchmark status
- **Source:** https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2010/2010.02.11/

- **Reviewer affiliation:** University of Pennsylvania
- **Verdict:** Strong endorsement

## EMILY WILSON (2017)

**Full title:** The Odyssey  
**Publisher:** W. W. Norton, 2017  
**Pages:** 582  
**ISBN:** 978-0-393-08905-9  
**Meter/Style:** Iambic pentameter, plain modern English, matches Homer's line count  
**Notes:** First complete English translation by a woman. NYT 100 Notable Books 2018. Substantial scholarly and public debate.

### Reviews

#### Pache, Corinne — BMCR 2018.10.58
- **Reviewer affiliation:** Trinity University, San Antonio
- **Verdict:** Largely positive
- **Strengths:**
  - Limpid style, "a quick-paced page-turner"
  - Sensitive metaphorical rendering (desire opening "like a sail"; χειρὶ παχείῃ as "muscular, firm hand")
  - Excellent introductory material and notes
  - "The most vivid … one wants to keep on reading"
- **Weaknesses:**
  - "Canapés" (for εἴδατα) and "mavericks" (for ἀθέμιστος) as jarring anachronisms
  - πολύτροπος as "complicated" forecloses the Greek's deliberate ambiguity
  - Avoiding formulaic repetitions undermines Homer's use of σήματα
  - Calls enslaved women "girls" seven times in 35 lines — contradicts stated aims on slavery language
- **Overall:** "An amazing achievement, a thrill to read, and the best English translation … sturdier than the original: a bit less open-ended, less polytropic, less complicated."
- **Source:** https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2018/2018.10.58/

#### Whitaker, Richard — *Acta Classica* 2020
- **Reviewer affiliation:** University of Cape Town
- **Verdict:** Critical / negative
- **Strengths:** [none noted]
- **Weaknesses:**
  - Systematic distortion: omits "though he longed to" when Odysseus "failed to keep them safe," removing the moral nuance of men perishing "through their own recklessness"
  - Renders πεντηκόσιοι as "fifty" instead of "five hundred"
  - Misattributes speeches; creates phantom characters by splitting single individuals
  - Applies "slave" uniformly across ἀμφίπολος, θεράπων, οἰνόχοος — collapses important social distinctions
  - Tonal incoherence: "babysit" and "whooshed" alongside "vernal Dawn"
  - Tries to modernise Homer, critique its values, and serve as classroom text simultaneously — succeeds at none
- **Overall:** "Wilson's revisionist approach, while innovative, undermines academic credibility through systematic distortions, numerous errors, and inconsistent application of interpretive choices."
- **Source:** https://www.academia.edu/97598353/HOMERS_ODYSSEY_THREE_WAYS_RECENT_TRANSLATIONS

#### Johnson, Marguerite — *Australian Book Review*, December 2018
- **Reviewer affiliation:** University of Newcastle / Honorary, University of Queensland
- **Verdict:** Enthusiastic
- **Key arguments:**
  - Groundbreaking as first major English translation by a woman
  - Maintains fidelity to Homer's structure while achieving readability
  - Epithet flexibility ("complicated," "the man who can adapt to anything," "the lord of lies") shows interpretive sophistication
  - Superior to Peter Green's Iliad in poetic quality
- **Overall:** "A translation of her selected text that is without peer … may be the definitive modern English Odyssey for contemporary readers."
- **Source:** https://www.academia.edu/39297440/Marguerite_Johnson_review_The_Odyssey_translated_by_Emily_Wilson_The_

#### Burrow, Colin — *London Review of Books*, 2018
- **Reviewer affiliation:** All Souls College, Oxford
- **Verdict:** Appreciative with reservations
- **Key arguments:**
  - Wilson characterised as a skilled "moderniser"
  - Epithet translations work as "running commentary on emotional and social relationships"
  - Acknowledged the immense challenge of matching Homer's line count
- **Source:** LRB (issue date not retrieved)

#### Mason, Wyatt — *New York Times Magazine*, 2017
- **Verdict:** Highly positive
- **Key arguments:**
  - "Floored" by the opening lines
  - Praised alliterative, musical, "natural and subtle" treatment
  - Highlighted Wilson's "radically contemporary voice"
- **Source:** NYT Magazine

#### Balmer, Josephine — *New Statesman*, 2018
- **Reviewer affiliation:** Translator and classicist
- **Verdict:** Positive
- **Key arguments:**
  - Describes Wilson as a "careful and creative scholar"
  - Dismantles the myth that only established poets can translate epic
- **Source:** New Statesman

## PETER GREEN (2018)

**Full title:** The Odyssey: A New Translation  
**Publisher:** University of California Press, 2018  
**Pages:** 522  
**Meter/Style:** Six-beat accentual line; allows near-complete rendering of Greek originals  
**Notes:** Green is a major classical scholar (author of *Alexander of Macedon*). Introduction engages heavily with 19th-century Analytic Homeric scholarship.

### Reviews

#### Whitaker, Richard — *Acta Classica* 2020
- **Reviewer affiliation:** University of Cape Town
- **Verdict:** Second-best of the three translations compared (better than Wilson, behind Verity's intro)
- **Strengths:**
  - "Weight and dignity" — captures complex emotional landscapes
  - Six-beat line allows near-complete rendering
  - Best overall balance of readability and linguistic sensitivity
- **Weaknesses:**
  - Introduction too steeped in 19th-century Analytic scholarship for undergraduates
  - Archaic vocabulary: "wroth with," "Zeus forfend," "raiment," "gaily"
  - Casual sexism: frames Calypso episode as "a case of the seven-year itch"
- **Overall:** "Strongest overall, balancing readability with sensitivity to language."
- **Source:** https://www.academia.edu/97598353/HOMERS_ODYSSEY_THREE_WAYS_RECENT_TRANSLATIONS

## A. VERITY (2016)

**Full title:** The Odyssey  
**Publisher:** Oxford University Press (Oxford World's Classics), 2016  
**Pages:** 354  
**Intro:** William Allan  
**Meter/Style:** Plain, direct prose  
**Notes:** Explicitly pedagogical. Allan's introduction considered best available for newcomers.

### Reviews

#### Whitaker, Richard — *Acta Classica* 2020
- **Verdict:** Reliable but dull
- **Strengths:**
  - William Allan's introduction "precisely calibrated for newcomers" — pedagogically exemplary
  - Reliable accuracy in "mostly plain, direct English"
  - "Earns the reader's respect as a careful, straightforward rendering"
- **Weaknesses:**
  - "Produces no fireworks" — often a little dull
  - Occasional archaic constructions: "what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth"
  - Lacks vitality to enthuse first-time readers
- **Source:** https://www.academia.edu/97598353/HOMERS_ODYSSEY_THREE_WAYS_RECENT_TRANSLATIONS

#### Pedrick, Victoria — BMCR 2018.07.37
- **Reviewer affiliation:** Department of Classics, Georgetown University
- **Verdict:** Positive with qualifications
- **Strengths:**
  - Good ear for tonal shifts between archaic and contemporary idiom (e.g. Book 2.96: "Young princes, my suitors!" captures nuance well)
  - Effectively captures "the intricacies and occasional ungainliness of Homer's poetic style"
  - Consistent, readable; "succeeds very well" in stated goals
- **Weaknesses:**
  - Handles δῖος ("godlike"/"holy") problematically — overemphasises divine qualities where mortal excellence is intended
  - Renders θεός as "god" rather than "the god," inadvertently suggesting monotheism
  - Introduction "underwhelming, too little for the first-time reader" [note: contrast with Whitaker, who praises Allan's intro highly]
  - Single map omits geographically significant locations
- **Overall:** Works excellently for casual and high-school readers; utility for college-level study is questionable; competing Shewring edition offers superior value
- **Source:** https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2018/2018.07.37/

## GEORGE CHAPMAN (1616)

**Full title:** The Odysses of Homer  
**Publisher:** Nathaniel Butter, London, 1616  
**Meter/Style:** Iambic pentameter (Odyssey); earlier Iliad in fourteeners  
**Notes:** The translation immortalised by Keats's sonnet "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer." Chapman did not read Greek fluently and worked partly from Spondanus's Greek-Latin edition and Andreas Divus's Latin translation. His interpretive framework reads Odysseus as a Stoic moral hero — "the mind's inward, constant and unconquerd Empire." Academic treatment is literary-historical rather than classical-philological.

### Reviews / Scholarly Discussion

#### Burrow, Colin — *London Review of Books*, Vol. 24 No. 12, 2002
- **Reviewer affiliation:** All Souls College, Oxford
- **Review title:** "Chapmaniac"
- **Verdict:** Admiring of the Iliad; critical of the Odyssey
- **Key arguments:**
  - The Odyssey's second half "translated in some haste, and with little sympathy, probably in the years 1611–15"
  - Chapman's allegorical lens — Odysseus as stoic hero — causes him to lose interest whenever Odysseus is "wily, mendacious, or shows inexplicable reserve"
  - Result: mechanical execution rather than inspired translation in the Odyssey's latter books
  - Defends Chapman against Matthew Arnold's dismissal of "Elizabethan fancifulness": his approach is superior to "timid, reverent and excessively archaeological" modern translations
  - Final assessment: "not quite as great as Keats says he is"
- **Source:** https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v24/n12/colin-burrow/chapmaniac

#### Swinburne, Algernon Charles (19th century)
- **Verdict:** Celebratory
- **Key quote:** "Romantic and sometimes barbaric grandeur … freshness, strength, and inextinguishable fire"

#### Academic note
- Princeton UP reissued a critical edition (ed. Allardyce Nicoll) — Chapman's Homer: The Iliad, The Odyssey — which remains the scholarly reference text
- Modern academic discussion focuses on Chapman's Stoic reading and his use of Latin intermediaries rather than on the translation as a practical rendering of Homer

## ALEXANDER POPE (1725–26)

**Full title:** The Odyssey of Homer  
**Publisher:** Bernard Lintot, London, 1725–26 (5 vols.); partly co-translated with William Broome and Elijah Fenton  
**Meter/Style:** Heroic couplets  
**Notes:** A literary monument rather than a scholarly translation. Matthew Arnold's 1861 lecture "On Translating Homer" attacked Pope directly, arguing his version replaced Homer's plainness and speed with ornate Augustan artifice. Pope's Odyssey is now studied as 18th-century English poetry, not as access to Homer.

### Scholarly Discussion

#### Arnold, Matthew — "On Translating Homer," 1861
- **Key argument:** Pope's "nobleness" is not Homer's nobleness — Pope is "literary" where Homer is "plain"; his ornate couplets betray Homer's speed and directness
- **Legacy:** Arnold's lecture defined the terms of translation criticism for a century and was explicitly the frame through which all subsequent translators (Lattimore, Rieu, Fagles, Wilson) positioned themselves

## E. V. RIEU (1946)

**Full title:** The Odyssey  
**Publisher:** Penguin Books, 1946 (Penguin Classics No. 1); revised by D. C. H. Rieu, 2003  
**Pages:** ~368  
**Meter/Style:** Plain English prose; explicitly novelistic — Rieu wanted to render Homer as "a novel"  
**Notes:** Founded the Penguin Classics series; sold over 3 million copies. Rieu was himself the series editor until 1964. Revised edition (2003) by his son D. C. H. Rieu with intro by Peter V. Jones corrects some embellishments.

### Reviews / Scholarly Discussion

#### Blackfriars review — June 1946
- **Reviewer:** "W.S." (unattributed initials)
- **Publication:** *Blackfriars*, Vol. 27, Issue 315, pp. 237–238
- **Verdict:** [Full text paywalled — Cambridge Core]
- **Source:** DOI 10.1017/S1754201400049432

#### Vanderschelden, Isabelle — *The Translator*, Vol. 20 No. 2, 2014
- **Publication:** Taylor & Francis / *The Translator* (peer-reviewed translation studies journal)
- **Article title:** "Popularising Homer: E. V. Rieu's English prose translations"
- **Key arguments:**
  - Rieu criticised archaising translation for restricting access to general readers; translated in an "egalitarian spirit" shaped by the aftermath of WWII
  - Explicitly rejected scholar-to-scholar translation: "In the past there has been too much translation by scholars for scholars, resulting in a weird kind of Greek-English"
  - Approach follows "dynamic equivalence" (thought-for-thought); regularly embroidered Homer's verse rather than translating it
  - Situated within a broader post-war democratisation of classical access
- **Source:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13556509.2014.968990

#### General scholarly reception
- Positive: recognised as a "classic itself" and praised for making Homer genuinely readable for the first time by a mass audience
- Critical: classical scholars noted systematic embellishment and that the novelistic frame distorts the poem's oral, formulaic character — the very qualities Carne-Ross defended in Lattimore, Rieu suppresses
- The Rieu/Verity succession at Oxford World's Classics is telling: OUP replaced Rieu with Shewring (1980) and later Verity (2016), suggesting institutional preference for greater fidelity

## WALTER SHEWRING (1980)

**Full title:** The Odyssey  
**Publisher:** Oxford University Press, 1980 (Oxford World's Classics)  
**Meter/Style:** Prose; close to the spirit of the Greek without archaising  
**Intro:** G. S. Kirk (leading Homeric scholar; author of *The Songs of Homer*)  
**Notes:** OUP's replacement for Rieu. Shewring appended a 30-page "Epilogue on Translation" tracing English Odyssey translations from Chapman onward and explaining his own choices. Praised by classicists as more reliable than Rieu. Later superseded at OUP by Verity (2016).

### Reviews / Scholarly Discussion

#### Kirk, G. S. — Introduction, OUP 1980
- **Reviewer affiliation:** Regius Professor of Greek, Cambridge; foremost Homeric scholar of his generation
- **Key argument:** Introduction emphasises the oral origins of Homeric poetry — positions Shewring's translation as responsive to these origins in a way prose prose can achieve
- **Note:** Kirk's presence as introducer is itself a scholarly endorsement

#### Goldstein, D. M. — BMCR 2001.08.39 (indirect)
- **Key argument:** The Shewring edition offered "superior value at lower cost" compared to Hammond's prose translation (Duckworth, 2000) — cited as the better prose alternative for students
- **Source:** https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2001/2001.08.39/

#### General reception
- Described as coming "as close to the spirit of the original Greek as our language will allow"
- Praised as "one of the few really outstanding versions of Homer's famous epic poem"
- Classicists cited it over Rieu as the more faithful prose option, though it never achieved Rieu's popular reach

## STEPHEN MITCHELL (2013)

**Full title:** The Odyssey: (The Stephen Mitchell Translation)  
**Publisher:** Atria Books (Simon & Schuster), 2013  
**Meter/Style:** Verse; colloquial modern English; "muscular language close to spoken English"  
**Notes:** Mitchell does not read ancient Greek — his translations across multiple languages (Gilgamesh, Iliad, Tao Te Ching, Beowulf, etc.) are based on existing translations and scholarly sources rather than the original. This has been the central point of academic controversy. He had previously translated the Iliad (2011) to similar criticism.

### Reviews / Scholarly Discussion

#### Academic controversy: the non-Hellenist translator
- Mitchell is a "translator" in the literary sense rather than the philological: he works from existing English translations, interlinears, and secondary scholarship
- This is openly acknowledged but has drawn consistent criticism from classicists who argue it produces a re-rendering of other translators' choices rather than Homer's Greek
- BMCR did not review the Mitchell Odyssey — its editorial policy prioritises translations by scholars with Greek — which is itself a signal of standing in the classical academy
- The controversy mirrors that surrounding his Iliad (Free Press, 2011), which received similarly mixed academic reception

#### General literary reception
- Praised by writers and general reviewers for readability and rhythmic drive
- Publisher claims it was "the first translation to benefit from modern advances in textual scholarship" — a claim contested by classicists
- Described as excellent for first-time readers; not recommended as a scholarly text

### Colin Burrow — deepened / LRB April 26, 2018
- **Reviewer affiliation:** All Souls College, Oxford
- **Article title:** "Light through the Fog: The End of the Epithet"
- **Verdict:** Appreciative with reservations (but markedly positive on craftsmanship)
- **Key arguments:**
  - Wilson's epithet strategy transforms Homer's repeated formulas into "a range of phrases which provide a running commentary on emotional and social relationships" (πολύμητις becomes variously "cunningly," "warily," "the trickster," "the mastermind" depending on emotional context)
  - Her compression from ~1/3 syllable reduction of Homer's hexameter to iambic pentameter requires "sharp and sometimes simplifying decisions" — but Burrow praises the insights this yields
  - Beautiful descriptive passages: "Her voice was beautiful. Around the cave / a luscious forest flourished..." and "It was full of wings" for Homer's descriptive language
  - Translation "full of insights" regarding "emotional relationships and implicit interchanges between characters"
  - Offers "a perceptive reading of *The Odyssey*" that rewards careful study
- **Weaknesses acknowledged:** Some epithets lose Greek's deliberate ambiguity; compression can simplify implications
- **Key quotes:**

## WILSON'S TRANSLATOR'S INTRODUCTION — HER OWN DEFENCE

Emily Wilson's introduction to her *Odyssey* translation (79 pages in the Norton edition, 2017) serves as a primary source documenting her translation philosophy across five key areas:

### On Slavery Vocabulary
- Wilson states her principle of uniform translation for enslaved characters: uses "slave" consistently for δμῶαι (dmōai) and related terms, rather than euphemistic alternatives like "maid" or "servant"
- Rationale: makes visible the historical reality of enslavement and sexual coercion; refuses to soften Homer's depiction of enslaved women through sanitised language
- Her introduction draws analogy to ante-bellum American plantation slavery to underscore the reality of what Homer's text describes
- This choice invites "genuine empathy" and prevents readers from overlooking the "horrific" sexual violence depicted in the text

### On Epithets
- Wilson rejects the "fossil" approach: earlier translators (especially 20th-century) locked formulaic epithets in place to mimic Greek repetition (e.g., always "wine-dark sea," always "grey-eyed Athena")
- Her principle: translate epithets contextually, varying them to provide "running commentary on emotional and social relationships" (Burrow's phrase, echoing Wilson's own explanation)
- Examples from introduction: πολύμητις (polymētis, "many wiles") becomes "complicated," "wily," "the trickster," "the mastermind," "the man who can adapt to anything" — each chosen for emotional resonance with Odysseus's state or action at that moment
- Purpose: makes ancient character psychology legible to modern readers; reveals Homer's *intent* (emotional and moral complexity) rather than mimicking his *form* (repetition)

### On Metre (Iambic Pentameter)
- Wilson selected iambic pentameter because it is "the conventional metre for English verse" — citing precedent in Chaucer, Shakespeare, Milton, Byron, Keats
- Rationale for this metre: best preserves "regular music" that Homer's hexameter would have provided; makes the text readable aloud; avoids the "sterile homework-like quality" of some modern prose translations
- She expresses frustration that most contemporary translations abandon metre, preventing students from experiencing "Homeric poetry as always rhythmical, inviting reading out loud, speaking to the body, pulsing with a regular music"
- Her introduction emphasises that "all translations are interpretations" — metre is not transparent access to Homer, but rather a deliberate artistic choice that shapes reading experience

### On Line-Count (12,110 lines)
- Wilson's primary technical achievement: maintained the original's line count *exactly* in iambic pentameter (12,110 lines in both Greek and English)
- Rationale: preserves Homer's pace and narrative rhythm; resists the temptation to expand (as many translators do) or compress
- This constraint is extraordinarily difficult because English requires more syllables than Greek to express the same meaning, and iambic pentameter (ten syllables per line) contains fewer syllables than Greek dactylic hexameter (~seventeen syllables per line)
- Her introduction presents this as a deliberate Oulipian-style restriction that forces "sharp and sometimes simplifying decisions about which of Homer's implications to make explicit" (Burrow) — but yielding superior narrative drive
- Multiple reviewers cite this achievement as remarkable: Madeline Miller, Wyatt Mason, and others highlight it as technically extraordinary

### On Sexual Violence and Women's Voices
- Wilson's introduction addresses Homer's depiction of sexual violence, slavery, and women's agency directly
- She refuses to present female characters as ridiculous or pitiful; maintains Homer's "psychologically truthful vision" that generates "deep understanding and sympathy with everyone"
- Specific interventions:
  - Penelope's hands rendered as "muscular" (master weaver's hands) rather than "delicate" — honours her craft rather than sexualising her
  - Enslaved women called "slaves" not softened terminology — prevents erasure of their coerced status and sexual vulnerability
  - Calypso presented with dignity, not as a seductress or obstacle — faithful to Homer's characterisation
- Her introduction argues that previous (male) translators' misogynistic choices were *interpretive*, not inevitable — her role is to offer an alternative interpretation that treats women's voices and suffering as central to the epic's moral weight

### On Anachronism
- Wilson's introduction does not deny modernisation (e.g., "strapping single men," "pep talk," "canapés") but positions it as *consistent* with Homer's own practice of updating traditional stories with contemporary details
- She argues that maintaining accessibility for contemporary readers—avoiding archaic language that distances modern students—is itself faithful to Homer's *purpose*: entertainment and emotional engagement
- Her introduction cites Homer's use of contemporary technology and social details in the *Odyssey* (references to iron weapons, contemporary ship-building) as evidence that Homer himself was not archaising

## THE LINE-COUNT ACHIEVEMENT — VERIFICATION AND IMPACT

### The Numbers
- **Homer's Odyssey:** 12,110 lines in Greek (dactylic hexameter)
- **Wilson's Odyssey:** 12,110 lines in English (iambic pentameter)
- **Wilson's stated constraint:** matched line-for-line, no padding or compression

### Critical Commentary on This Achievement

**From reviewers:**
- Wyatt Mason (NYT Magazine) highlights this as part of the extraordinary technical feat
- Madeline Miller (Washington Post): "impressively limiting herself to the same number of lines as Homer's original" — notes this as rare and remarkable
- Colin Burrow (LRB): acknowledges "the challenging task of translating the poem into the same number of iambic pentameter lines as there are hexameters in the original," noting the compression required (approximately one-third syllable reduction from Greek to English line)
- Chicago Review of Books: praises Wilson for refusing to expand, thereby "preserving narrative pace"
- Multiple sources note this as an almost Oulipian constraint that forces difficult choices but yields superior narrative drive

### Why This Matters
- The line-count match is presented by Wilson and reviewers as a *technical achievement* (not mere gimmick): it forces translation discipline that prevents expansion, maintains Homer's narrative momentum, and (per Burrow) yields "insights" about emotional relationships
- It distinguishes Wilson's translation from competitors (Fagles, Lattimore, Lombardo all expand considerably; Fitzgerald uses flexible blank verse)
- It positions Wilson in a tradition of poet-translators who embrace formal constraint (similar to modernist translation theory)
