The four translators below (Robert Fagles, Robert Fitzgerald, Daniel Mendelsohn, and Emily Wilson) are the ones with full reference texts on hand for line-by-line comparison. Emily Wilson's is the only one this piece argues for.

01.

The complicated man

Every other translator chose a side — heroic, or evasive. Wilson is the only one who held the ambiguity Homer wrote.

Od. 1.1 πολύτροπος Literal: "of many turns / ways." Simultaneously: cunning, resourceful, experienced — and devious, slippery, unreliable. The poem's first word about Odysseus. Homer refuses to tell you which reading is right. ◆ green = what Wilson preserves · yellow = what the others lose
Robert Fitzgerald1961
Near-universal praise

Sing in me, Muse, and through me tell the story

of that man skilled in all ways of contending,

the wanderer, harried for years on end,

after he plundered the stronghold

on the proud height of Troy.

["skilled in all ways" — heroic only; the moral critique vanishes]

Robert Fagles1996
Most widely taught

Sing to me of the man, Muse, the man of twists and turns

driven time and again off course, once he had plundered

the hallowed heights of Troy.

Many cities of men he saw and learned their minds,

many pains he suffered, heartsick on the open sea…

["twists and turns" — spatial and physical; moral weight absent]

Daniel Mendelsohn2025
Early strong reception

Tell me the tale of a man, Muse, who had so many roundabout ways

To wander, driven off course, after sacking Troy's hallowed keep;

Many the peoples whose cities he saw and whose ways of thinking he learned,

Many the toils he suffered at sea, anguish in his heart…

["roundabout ways" — evasive, but still spatial; loses the moral judgement]

Emily Wilson2017
Landmark; deeply contested

Tell me about a complicated man.

Muse, tell me how he wandered and was lost

when he had wrecked the holy town of Troy,

and where he went, and who he met, the pain

he suffered in the storms at sea…

["complicated" — both heroic and morally questionable, simultaneously]

Wyatt Mason, writing in the NYT Magazine in 2017, was "floored" by this opening. He was right to be. πολύτροπος is genuinely ambivalent in Greek: it can mean cunning and resourceful (a compliment), or devious and slippery (a warning). Every other translator forecloses that ambiguity by choosing one reading. "Skilled in all ways of contending" (Fitzgerald) lands entirely on heroism. "Twists and turns" (Fagles) is vivid but physical, not moral. "Roundabout ways" (Mendelsohn) is evasive without moral charge. "Complicated" is the only English word that holds both meanings at once: admirable and questionable, experienced and untrustworthy. Homer didn't tell us which reading was correct because he meant both.

Even Corinne Pache, who flags "complicated" as a weakness in her BMCR review, concedes the point: Wilson's translation is "sturdier than the original: a bit less open-ended, less polytropic, less complicated." Less complicated than πολύτροπος — which is still a richer reading than "man of twists and turns."

Wyatt Mason · NYT Magazine, Nov 2, 2017 · Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58
02.

Every epithet, every time

Homer's formulas weren't decoration. Wilson translates what they do — and at Od. 9.1, the trickster announces himself before beginning four books of possibly tall tales.

Od. 9.1 πολύμητις · τὸν δ' ἀπαμειβόμενος προσέφη πολύμητις Ὀδυσσεύς "Then in reply resourceful Odysseus addressed him." πολύμητις = "of many counsels / wiles / resources." The standard epithet for Odysseus's intelligence. Here he is about to speak for four consecutive books (9–12) — the most extended, and most questionable, narrative in the epic.
Robert Fitzgerald1961
Near-universal praise

Resourceful Odysseus answered him and said:

"Alcinous, king and admiration of men,

how beautiful this is, to hear a minstrel

gifted as yours…

[Od. 9.19–20] I am Laertes' son, Odysseus.

Men hold me formidable for guile

in peace and war: this fame has gone abroad."

Robert Fagles1996
Most widely taught

The great tactician answered, turning to him:

"Alcinous, majesty, shining among your island people,

what a fine thing it is to listen to such a bard…

[Od. 9.19–20] I am Odysseus, son of Laertes,

known to the world for every kind of craft —

my fame goes up to the heavens."

Daniel Mendelsohn2025
Early strong reception

Then much-contriving Odysseus answered him:

"Alcinous, lord of all, great among your people,

truly it is a fine thing to hear a singer such as this…

[Od. 9.19–20] I am Odysseus, son of Laertes,

who am known among all men for my craftiness;

my fame goes up to heaven."

Emily Wilson2017
Landmark; deeply contested

Then wily Odysseus, the lord of lies, replied:

"Alcinous, great among all peoples, truly

it is a lovely thing to hear a singer…

[Od. 9.19–20] I am Odysseus, Laertes' son.

All the world knows me: my tricks, my guile.

My fame reaches to heaven."

At Od. 9.1, Odysseus is about to speak for the next four books — the Cyclops, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, Circe, the underworld. These are the most famous passages in the epic, and also the most self-serving: Odysseus is his own narrator. Wilson's "the lord of lies" is not a mistranslation of πολύμητις — it is a contextually precise rendering of the epithet's resonance at this particular moment. Homer's epithet system did structural work in oral performance. "Resourceful" (Fitzgerald) or "great tactician" (Fagles) remain constant across all occurrences, ignoring that resonance. Wilson varies the epithet to provide, as Colin Burrow observed, "a running commentary on emotional and social relationships."

Colin Burrow · LRB Vol. 40 No. 8, April 26, 2018 · Marguerite Johnson · Australian Book Review, December 2018

03.

One line for one line

Homer's Odyssey is 12,110 lines. Wilson's English translation is 12,110 lines, in iambic pentameter. No other major translator even attempted it.

Od. 1.1–10 the proem · ten Greek lines The opening ten lines of the Odyssey. Count the lines each translator uses for the same Greek span. Fitzgerald's blank verse expands. Mendelsohn's six-beat line produces fewer but far longer lines. Wilson's iambic pentameter: ten lines for ten.

of that man skilled in all ways of contending,

the wanderer, harried for years on end,

after he plundered the stronghold

on the proud height of Troy.

He saw the townlands

and learned the minds of many distant men,

and weathered many bitter nights and days

in his deep heart at sea, while he fought only

to save his life, to bring his shipmates home.

But not by will nor valor could he save them,

for their own recklessness destroyed them all —

children and fools, they killed and feasted on

the cattle of Lord Hēlios, the Sun…

Emily Wilson2017
Landmark; deeply contested

Tell me about a complicated man.

Muse, tell me how he wandered and was lost

when he had wrecked the holy town of Troy,

and where he went, and who he met, the pain

he suffered in the storms at sea, and how

he worked to save his life and bring his men

back home. He failed to keep them safe; poor fools,

they ate the Sun God's cattle, and the god

kept them from home. Now goddess, child of Zeus,

tell us their story, starting anywhere.

10 lines for 10 Greek lines — iambic pentameter throughout

Wilson is the only translator of a major modern English Odyssey to maintain a strict line-for-line correspondence with Homer across the entire poem. Colin Burrow identifies this as the discipline that generates her translation's insights: it forces "sharp and sometimes simplifying decisions about which of Homer's implications to make explicit" — and those decisions, compelled by the constraint, often produce the clearest possible rendering of what the Greek is actually doing. Madeline Miller, writing in the Washington Post, called the achievement "impressively limiting herself to the same number of lines as Homer's original" — rare in the history of English Homer.

Colin Burrow · LRB 2018 · Madeline Miller · Washington Post, Nov 16, 2017 · Wilson's translator's introduction, Norton 2017

04.

The music Homer had

Homer's hexameter was regular, rhythmical, meant to be sung. Wilson's iambic pentameter is the English equivalent. Most of her predecessors abandoned metre entirely.

Od. 5.61–68 ᾄδουσα · λίγ' ἀοιδῆς · ὕλη ἐπήρεφε Hermes arrives at Calypso's cave. The passage is a set-piece of Homeric descriptive writing: sounds, scents, birds, forest, springs. Colin Burrow (LRB 2018) singles out Wilson's rendering of this passage as an exemplar of her translation's musical quality.
Robert Fitzgerald1961
Near-universal praise

She had a voice; her spinning song rang out

upon the island. Tall trees grew in ranks

around the cave — sweet-smelling alder,

black poplar, scented cypress. There

the long-winged birds would nest: the horned owl,

the hawk, the cormorant roaming the salt sea.

A great vine trailed its clusters across the cave.

Four springs ran sparkling, side by side…

Robert Fagles1996

alder and black poplar, fragrant cypress.

and the chattering sea-crows who work the waves.

Right at the cave's mouth a vine ran wide,

heavy with grapes. Four springs ran clear and bright…

Emily Wilson2017
Landmark; deeply contested

A great fire burned, and cedar split on coals

spread fragrance through the island. She was singing:

her voice was beautiful. Around the cave

a luscious forest flourished: alder trees,

black poplar, fragrant cypress. Long-winged birds

had built their nests there: owls, and hawks, and those

dark-calling sea-crows. It was full of wings.

A great vine trailed the cave's broad mouth, in bloom,

heavy with grapes. Four close-set springs ran clear…

Read Wilson's lines aloud. "Her voice was beautiful. Around the cave / a luscious forest flourished." The sentence breaks exactly at the line-end, the stress falls naturally on every second syllable: her VOICE was BEAU-ti-FUL. a-ROUND the CAVE. This is what regular metre does — it makes the prose readable aloud, the way Homer's Greek was always meant to be heard. Wilson's introduction argues that most modern translations have abandoned this: "all the commonly-read versions … are lacking in metrical regularity" — the result is text that reads like heightened prose but lacks the pulse. Josephine Balmer (New Statesman, 2018) called Wilson's versification "fluid and immensely readable," blowing away "the cobwebs of pseudo-archaisms or epic pomposity."

Colin Burrow singles out Wilson's "It was full of wings" — three words for Homer's description of the cave's bird life — as the kind of sharp compression her formal constraint produces. Not "long-winged birds nested there," not "the long-winged birds made their nests": just the pure sensory fact, stripped to its feathers.

Colin Burrow · LRB Vol. 40 No. 8, April 26, 2018 · Josephine Balmer · New Statesman, January 21, 2018 · Wilson's translator's introduction, Norton 2017
05.

Not "maids" — slaves

The enslaved women were hanged after the suitor slaughter. Previous translators called them "maids." Wilson refuses the euphemism.

Od. 22.437–447 δμωαί · ἐϋπλέκτῳ βρόχῳ δμωαί = enslaved women, owned property of the household, subject to the sexual demands of whoever was in control. Telemachus hangs twelve of them after the suitor massacre because they had slept with the suitors. They were not given a choice. Wilson translates δμωαί as "slaves" throughout the poem — the only accurate English word for their legal and social status.
Robert Fitzgerald1961
Near-universal praise

"These women, then, shall have no clean death: not I,

who lay in the arms of young men while dishonor

fell on the Lady and their master's name."

the column of the hall's great dome, then swung it

up high so none could touch the ground.

Like thrushes caught in a net spread in the thicket,

each woman found her noose…

Madeline Miller (Washington Post, 2017) makes the argument directly: calling these women "maids" or "serving-women" prevents readers from registering "this really horrific thing that happened." These women were owned. Their sleeping with the suitors was not infidelity, not poor judgment, not a moral failing for which they bore any choice. It was coercion by the men who controlled the household during Odysseus's absence. Wilson's "slaves" is not an anachronism or a flattening — it is the only English word that makes their status, and therefore their death, fully visible.

Wilson's translator's introduction addresses this directly: she argues that earlier translators' softened vocabulary — "maids," "handmaidens," "serving-women," "girls" — imports a Downton Abbey register that Homer never intended. The Greek δμωαί denotes owned property. That word is available in English. Wilson uses it.

Madeline Miller · Washington Post, Nov 16, 2017 · Wilson's translator's introduction, Norton 2017 · Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58
06.

The women Homer saw

Penelope has been weaving for twenty years. Homer gives her a master craftsperson's hands. Most translators gave her softer ones.

Od. 21.5–8 χειρὶ παχείῃ "With her thick / strong hand." Penelope reaches for the great bow to set the contest that will decide her fate. παχύς = thick, stout, heavy — the hand of a skilled craftsperson, not a decorative aristocrat. Corinne Pache (BMCR 2018) singles this passage out: Wilson's rendering is "more striking" than any competitor's.
Robert Fitzgerald1961

gold, and iron, finely wrought…

She took the great bow down from off its peg

with both hands, still in its polished case,

and sat down there and laid it on her knees

and wept aloud for her husband far away.

Corinne Pache calls Wilson's "muscular, firm hand" for χειρὶ παχείῃ "more striking" than any competitor — and it is. Penelope is not an idle queen waiting by a window. She has been weaving for twenty years, unpicking by night what she wove by day. Her hands are a craftsperson's hands: thick, trained, strong. Fitzgerald's "both hands" neutralises the Greek. Fagles' "her own hands" and Mendelsohn's "broad hand" approach the Greek without getting there. Wilson's "muscular, firm hand" is the only rendering that honours what Homer actually wrote — and what it means for who Penelope is.

Madeline Miller flags the same passage: Wilson's Penelope has "muscular" hands that "honor the character's craft rather than sexualising her." This is not a small interpretive choice. It is the difference between a decorative character and a woman whose body has been shaped by twenty years of survival.

Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58 · Madeline Miller · Washington Post, Nov 16, 2017 · Wilson's translator's introduction, Norton 2017
And also: other women Wilson restores
▸ Calypso: goddess, not obstacle Od. 5.55–75 · θεὸς αὐδήεσσα

Earlier translators tend to frame Calypso as an obstacle to Odysseus's homecoming — a seductress who delays his return. Wilson's translation treats her as what the Greek calls her: a θεὸς αὐδήεσσα, a goddess who speaks. Her cave is described with genuine beauty ("Her voice was beautiful. Around the cave / a luscious forest flourished"). Her grief at losing Odysseus is rendered without diminishment. Wilson's introduction argues that previous (male) translators' tendency to flatten female characters into obstacles or victims is itself an interpretive choice — and one she deliberately refuses.

Wilson's translator's introduction, Norton 2017 · Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58
▸ Nausicaa: Wilson's "like a sail" simile Od. 6.99–101 · ἱστίῳ ἠύτε

When Nausicaa stands apart from her companions, Homer uses a simile comparing her to a poplar or a ship's mast. Wilson's rendering of the simile — "like a sail" — for the comparison of Nausicaa amid her companions conveys, in Corinne Pache's phrase, "the metaphorical register" of the Greek with striking clarity. Where other translations render the image as a static architectural comparison, Wilson catches the dynamism: a sail fills and moves; it is shaped by wind and direction. This is a Nausicaa who is going somewhere.

Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58
The honours

The critical case, set out

Wilson's translation was greeted as a landmark event by a wide range of critics, classicists, and novelists working with the classical tradition. The passages above are not the full case — they are six of the strongest specific arguments. The four critics below represent the range of that reception.

"Floored" by the opening lines. "Small but radical changes to the way many key scenes of the epic are presented." Highlighted Wilson's "radically contemporary voice" and her achievement of matching Homer's 12,110-line count in iambic pentameter as technically extraordinary.
