## PASSAGE A — Od. 1.1 — πολύτροπον: "complicated" vs alternatives

**Greek:** ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, πολύτροπον  
**Dispute (Pache):** Wilson's "complicated" is a single psychological adjective that forecloses the Greek's deliberate spatial/tactical/moral ambiguity. πολύτροπος means "of many turns/ways" — cunning *and* much-travelled simultaneously. Every other translator preserves at least the ambiguity of direction/skill. Wilson imports a modern register of interior complexity absent from the Greek.

### Wilson 2017

>>>Tell me about a complicated man.
Muse, tell me how he wandered and was lost
when he had wrecked the holy town of Troy,
and where he went, and who he met, the pain
he suffered in the storms at sea, and how
he worked to save his life and bring his men
back home. He failed to keep them safe; poor fools,
they ate the Sun God's cattle, and the god
kept them from home. Now goddess, child of Zeus,
tell the old story for our modern times.
Find the beginning. All the other Greeks
who had survived the brutal sack of Troy
sailed safely home to their own wives—except
this man alone.

### Fagles 1996

>>>Sing to me of the man, Muse, the man of twists and turns
>>>driven time and again off course, once he had plundered
>>>the hallowed heights of Troy.
Many cities of men he saw and learned their minds,
many pains he suffered, heartsick on the open sea,
fighting to save his life and bring his comrades home.
But he could not save them from disaster, hard as he strove —
the recklessness of their own ways destroyed them all,
the blind fools, they devoured the cattle of the Sun
and the Sungod wiped from sight the day of their return.
Launch out on his story, Muse, daughter of Zeus,
start from where you will — sing for our time too.
By now, all the survivors, all who avoided
headlong death were safe at home, escaped the wars and waves.
But one man alone . . .

### Fitzgerald 1961

>>>Sing in me, Muse, and through me tell the story
>>>of that man skilled in all ways of contending,
the wanderer, harried for years on end,
after he plundered the stronghold
on the proud height of Troy.
He saw the townlands
and learned the minds of many distant men,
and weathered many bitter nights and days
in his deep heart at sea, while he fought only
to save his life, to bring his shipmates home.
But not by will nor valor could he save them,
for their own recklessness destroyed them all —
children and fools, they killed and feasted on
the cattle of Lord Hēlios, the Sun,
and he who moves all day through heaven
took from their eyes the dawn of their return.
Of these adventures, Muse, daughter of Zeus,
tell us in our time, lift the great song again.

### Mendelsohn 2025

>>>Tell me the tale of a man, Muse, who had so many roundabout ways
>>>To wander, driven off course, after sacking Troy's hallowed keep;
Many the peoples whose cities he saw and whose ways of thinking he learned,
Many the toils he suffered at sea, anguish in his heart
As he struggled to safeguard his life and the homecoming of his companions.
But he did not save his companions even so, though he longed to,
For their heedlessness destroyed them, theirs and nobody else's —
Fools that they were, like children, who devoured the sun-god Hyperion's
Cattle, and so he took from them the day of their homecoming.
Goddess, start where you will; daughter of Zeus, share the tale with us too.
Now all of the other men — those who'd fled looming destruction —
Were home again, escaped from the war or saved from the depths of the sea,
Except for him. Though he yearned to come home, though he yearned for his wife,
A nymph — the Lady Kalypso, that radiant goddess — kept him
Back in her smooth-hollowed caves, burning to make him her husband.

## PASSAGE B — Od. 1.5–9 — ἀτασθαλίῃσιν + νήπιοι + ἱέμενός περ

**Greek (5–6):** ἀλλ' οὐδ' ὧς ἑτάρους ἐρρύσατο, **ἱέμενός περ** / αὐτῶν γὰρ σφετέρῃσιν **ἀτασθαλίῃσιν** ὄλοντο, **νήπιοι**  
**Dispute 1 (Whitaker):** Wilson omits "though he longed to" (ἱέμενός περ, line 5) — the phrase showing Odysseus *wanted* to save his men. Without it, his failure looks like indifference rather than tragic inadequacy.  
**Dispute 2 (Pache / Whitaker):** Wilson renders ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ("recklessness / presumption / hubris against divine order") + νήπιοι ("children, fools") as simply "poor fools" — omitting the named moral quality entirely. The proem's programmatic claim that the men died *by their own recklessness* disappears.

Muse, tell me how he wandered and was lost
when he had wrecked the holy town of Troy,
and where he went, and who he met, the pain

>>>He failed to keep them safe; poor fools,

tell the old story for our modern times.

## PASSAGE C — Od. 9.105–115 — ἀθέμιστος: "mavericks" vs "lawless"

**Greek:** οἳ δ' ὑπερφίαλοι καὶ ἀθέμιστοι (Cyclopes: "overbearing and without themis")  
**Dispute (Pache):** Wilson's "mavericks" (for ἀθέμιστος) is an anachronistic Americanism. Themis is the divine principle underpinning social and cosmic law; the Cyclopes' lack of it is a theological statement about their position outside civilised order. "Mavericks" imports a personality-quirk framing absent from the Greek.

## PASSAGE D — Social vocabulary: ἀμφίπολοι and δμωαί

**Dispute (Pache / Whitaker):** Wilson flattens Homer's precise social vocabulary in two opposite directions simultaneously:
1. She calls ἀμφίπολοι (free female attendants/companions) "slaves" — falsely enslaving women who are not slaves
2. She calls δμωαί (enslaved household women) "girls" — infantilising women who are in fact adult slaves

The net effect undermines Wilson's own stated feminist project on both ends. Mendelsohn consistently distinguishes; Fagles and Fitzgerald use neutral terms that preserve status without over-specifying.

### D1 — Od. 6.18–21: ἀμφίπολοι sleeping at Nausicaa's door

**Greek:** τῇ δὲ δύω ἀμφίπολοι Χαρίτεσσιν εἰκυῖαι / σταθμοῖιν ἑκάτερθε κάθευδον  
("Two amphipoloi like the Graces were sleeping, one on each side of the doorposts")  
**Note:** Nausicaa's attendants are free Phaeacian women — daughters of free citizens attending their princess at court. There is no textual basis for calling them slaves. The term ἀμφίπολος denotes a female attendant or companion, status unspecified or implicitly free in a royal Phaeacian context.

#### Wilson 2017

In this form grey-eyed Athena said to her…
She went inside the decorated bedroom where
the young princess, Nausicaa, was sleeping,
as lovely as a goddess.
>>>Slaves were sleeping outside her doorway,
>>>one on either side; two charming girls
>>>with all the Graces' gifts.
The shining doors were shut, but like the wind
the goddess reached the bed of Nausicaa…

#### Fagles 1996

She made her way to the gaily painted room
where a young girl lay asleep…
a match for the deathless gods in build and beauty,
Nausicaa, the daughter of generous King Alcinous.
>>>Two handmaids fair as the Graces slept beside her,
>>>flanking the two posts, with the gleaming doors closed.
But the goddess drifted through like a breath of fresh air,
rushed to the girl's bed and hovering close she spoke…

#### Fitzgerald 1961

[she] moved to the bedside of the girl, and grew
visible as the shipman Dymas' daughter,
a girl the princess' age, and her dear friend.
[opening lines of Book 6 — attendants described below at line 91:]
…off they went in a ground-covering trot,
>>>with princess, maids, and laundry drawn behind.

#### Mendelsohn 2025

She entered a richly appointed chamber
where a maiden was fast asleep,
A girl whose bloom and appearance were like those
of some goddess immortal:
Nausikáa, the daughter of great-hearted Alkínoös.
>>>Near her were two attendants whose beauty
>>>came from the Graces,
>>>One on each side of the door-posts.
The polished doors were shut tight, but like a breath of wind
the goddess rushed toward the maiden's bedstead…

### D4 — Od. 22.421–460: δμωαί at execution — "girls" for adult enslaved women (inverse distortion)

**Greek:** δμωαί ("enslaved women of the household")  
**Dispute (Pache):** Here the women ARE actually enslaved (δμωαί, a word that does mean slave), and Wilson calls them "girls." This inverts the error above: where she imposed slavery on free women, here she infantilises actual slaves. The execution scene — Homer's most morally confronting — is softened by a term that distances the reader from the women's adult status and the gravity of what is happening to them.

#### Wilson 2017

"Tell me now about the household women.
Which ones dishonor me? And which are pure?"
The slave who loved her master answered,
"Child, I will tell you exactly how things stand.
>>>In this house we have fifty female slaves
>>>whom we have trained to work, to card the wool…
>>>Twelve stepped away from honor: those twelve girls
>>>ignore me, and Penelope our mistress."
…"Call the women who made those treasonous plots while I was gone."
The old nurse did so. Walking through the hall,
>>>she called the girls. Meanwhile, Odysseus summoned
the herdsmen and Telemachus…
"Now we must start to clear the corpses out.
>>>The girls must help. Then clean my stately chairs
and handsome tables with sponges…
restore my halls to health: take out the girls
between the courtyard wall and the rotunda.
Hack at them with long swords, eradicate all life from them."
>>>Sobbing desperately the girls came, weeping…
>>>The girls picked up the trash and took it out.
…then led the girls outside and trapped them…
>>>just so the girls, their heads all in a row,
were strung up with the noose around their necks
to make their death an agony.

#### Fagles 1996

"March the women out of the great hall —
between the roundhouse and the courtyard's strong stockade —
and hack them with your swords, slash out all their lives…"
>>>The women crowded in, huddling all together…
>>>wailing convulsively, streaming live warm tears.
First they carried out the bodies of the dead…
Odysseus shouted commands himself, moving things along
>>>and they kept bearing out the bodies — they were forced.
…then marched the women out of the great hall…
crammed them into a dead end, no way out from there,
and stern Telemachus gave the men their orders:
>>>"No clean death for the likes of them, by god!
>>>Not from me — they showered abuse on my head, my mother's too!
>>>You sluts — the suitors' whores!"
>>>so the women's heads were trapped in a line,
>>>nooses yanking their necks up, one by one…

#### Mendelsohn 2025

…they brought the female slaves out of the strong-built Hall
to a place between the round-house and the flawlessly built courtyard fence,
Forcing them into a corner from which there was no escape.
Telémakhos, sensible lad, began to speak to them then:
>>>"Let the death that I inflict on these women not be a clean one!
>>>For they poured upon my head an endless stream of insults —
>>>And on my mother, too — while they were sleeping with the Suitors."
>>>So did the women hold their heads all in a row,
>>>and the nooses went round the necks of them all
>>>so they'd die a most piteous death.
For a time, their feet went on twitching; but not for very long.

**Notes:** Mendelsohn uses "female slaves" and "women" — accurate and adult. Fagles uses "women" throughout (Telemachus's insult "you sluts" is his characterization, not the narrator's). Wilson's "girls" appears at least eight times in ~40 lines, while the same passage also correctly uses "fifty female slaves" for the broader household — making the inconsistency within her own translation visible.

## PASSAGE E — Od. 4.55–60 — εἴδατα + ταμίη: "canapés" and the "humble slave girl"

**Greek:** ταμίη δ' ἐπέθηκεν ἄρ' εἴδατα πολλά  
("the ταμίη set out many εἴδατα")  
**Dispute (Pache):** Wilson renders εἴδατα ("foods, delicacies" — a generic term) as "canapés." This is a French loanword for a specific type of cocktail party food, coined in the 18th century. Anachronistic and class-inflected in a way the Greek is not.  
**Additional issue:** The ταμίη is a housekeeper with authority over the storerooms — a position of responsibility, not menial servitude. Wilson calls her "a humble slave girl," which both mislabels the role and adds a deprecating adjective absent from Homer.

### Wilson 2017

A house girl brought a basin made of silver, and water in a golden jug.
She poured it over their hands to wash,
then set a table of polished wood beside them,
>>>and a humble slave girl brought bread and many canapés,
>>>a lavish spread.
The carver carried platters with every kind of meat,
and set before them cups made of gold.
Then ruddy Menelaus welcomed them both and told them,
"Help yourselves! Enjoy the food!"

### Fagles 1996

A maid brought water soon in a graceful golden pitcher
and over a silver basin tipped it out
so they might rinse their hands,
then pulled a gleaming table to their side.
>>>A staid housekeeper brought on bread to serve them,
>>>appetizers aplenty too, lavish with her bounty.
As a carver lifted platters of meat toward them,
meats of every sort, and set before them golden cups,
the red-haired king Menelaus greeted both guests…

### Fitzgerald 1961

### Mendelsohn 2025

A house-maid brought in water, pouring it out from a ewer —
Exquisite, wrought in gold — over a basin of silver
For them to wash their hands; then placed a table nearby.
>>>Then the housekeeper, worthy woman, brought in the bread and served it,
>>>Glad to have so many dishes to set out from her storerooms inside.
Hoisting his trenchers aloft, a steward set out all kinds
Of meats, and there among them he set down the goblets of gold.
Greeting the pair, Menelaos the Fair-Haired now addressed them:
"Enjoy…"

**Notes:** Fagles's "staid housekeeper" and Mendelsohn's "housekeeper, worthy woman" both reflect the ταμίη's administrative authority. Wilson's "humble slave girl" introduces servility and low status the Greek term does not carry. Mendelsohn's "so many dishes" and Fagles's "appetizers aplenty" keep εἴδατα generic; Wilson's "canapés" imports a specific class register from 18th-century French cookery.
