The four translators below (Robert Fagles, Robert Fitzgerald, Daniel Mendelsohn, and Emily Wilson) are the ones with full reference texts on hand for line-by-line comparison. Emily Wilson's is the only one this piece argues against.

01.

Did Odysseus even try to save his men?

Emily Wilson cut the line that says he did, and the line that named the men's own recklessness as the reason they died.

Od. 1.5–9 ἀτασθαλίῃσιν · ἱέμενός περ · νήπιοι "Yet not even so could he save his companions, though he longed to; for through their own reckless folly they perished: children, fools." The line tells us Odysseus tried; his men died from a named moral failing.
Robert Fitzgerald1961
Near-universal praise

in his deep heart at sea, while he fought only

to save his life, to bring his shipmates home.

But not by will nor valor could he save them,

for their own recklessness destroyed them all —

children and fools, they killed and feasted on

the cattle of Lord Hēlios, the Sun…

Robert Fagles1996
Most widely taught

many pains he suffered, heartsick on the open sea,

fighting to save his life and bring his comrades home.

But he could not save them from disaster, hard as he strove —

the recklessness of their own ways destroyed them all,

the blind fools, they devoured the cattle of the Sun…

Daniel Mendelsohn2025
Early strong reception

Many the toils he suffered at sea, anguish in his heart

As he struggled to safeguard his life and the homecoming of his companions.

But he did not save his companions even so, though he longed to,

For their heedlessness destroyed them, theirs and nobody else's —

Fools that they were, like children, who devoured the sun-god Hyperion's

Cattle…

Emily Wilson2017
Landmark; deeply contested

…the pain he suffered in the storms at sea, and how

he worked to save his life and bring his men

back home.

He failed to keep them safe; poor fools,

they ate the Sun God's cattle, and the god

kept them from home.

Where the Greek says ἀλλ' οὐδ' ὧς ("yet not even so," despite his efforts) Odysseus could not save his men, Emily Wilson leaves out this important qualification. And then, with breathtaking insouciance, she simply omits to translate the key phrase "though he longed to" (ἱέμενός περ, Od. 1.6) as well as, in its entirety, the crucial line: "it was through their own blind recklessness that they perished" (Od. 1.7).

Where the Greek says the sun god actively "took away" (ἀφείλετο, 1.9) their homecoming (i.e. destroyed the men because of their recklessness), Emily Wilson obscures this with her misleading "kept them from home." Without it, Odysseus's failure looks like indifference rather than tragic inadequacy, and the men die for no named reason.

Note the alignment between Robert Fitzgerald (1961), Robert Fagles (1996), and Daniel Mendelsohn (2025): three very different stylists all preserving the same two semantic loads (Odysseus's effort + the men's named moral fault) that Emily Wilson alone removes.

Richard Whitaker · Acta Classica 2020, p. 246
02.

Are these women slaves, or aristocrats?

One English word ("slave") for a dozen distinct Greek roles. Applied even to women who were free Phaeacian aristocrats.

Od. 6.18–21 ἀμφίπολοι Χαρίτεσσιν εἰκυῖαι "Two amphipoloi like the Graces were sleeping, one on each side of the doorposts." ἀμφίπολος = female attendant or companion (status unspecified, implicitly free in a royal context). Nausicaa's ἀμφίπολοι are daughters of free Phaeacian citizens.
Robert Fitzgerald1961
Near-universal praise

[Robert Fitzgerald compresses the doorway scene; the attendants are referenced at the wash-day procession line 91:]

…off they went in a ground-covering trot,

with princess, maids, and laundry drawn behind.

["maids" carries no slave register]

Robert Fagles1996
Most widely taught

She made her way to the gaily painted room

where a young girl lay asleep…

Nausicaa, the daughter of generous King Alcinous.

Two handmaids fair as the Graces slept beside her,

flanking the two posts, with the gleaming doors closed.

Daniel Mendelsohn2025
Early strong reception

She entered a richly appointed chamber

where a maiden was fast asleep…

Nausikáa, the daughter of great-hearted Alkínoös.

Near her were two attendants whose beauty

came from the Graces,

One on each side of the door-posts.

Emily Wilson2017
Landmark; deeply contested

She went inside the decorated bedroom where

the young princess, Nausicaa, was sleeping,

as lovely as a goddess.

Slaves were sleeping outside her doorway,

one on either side; two charming girls

with all the Graces' gifts.

Emily Wilson's "Pan-Slaveism" policy (her own description: she wants "to mark the fact that the idealized society depicted in the Odyssey is one where slavery is shockingly taken for granted") leads her to translate ἀμφίπολος as "slave" even when the women in question are free Phaeacian companions to a royal princess.

Richard Whitaker: Emily Wilson is simply wrong when she uses "slave" (as she often does) to render θεράπων ("attendant" or "squire") and κήρυξ ("herald"). But when her wrong-headed interpretation might get her into difficulties, she simply abandons it. At Od. 2.38 the herald Peisenor becomes "the competent official," and the Black African herald Eurybates (19.244) becomes "valet." The category buckles under its own internal contradictions.

Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58 · Richard Whitaker · Acta Classica 2020, p. 248
Three more passages where the same word triggers the same problem
▸ The ball scene: a "slave girl" misses the catch Od. 6.122 · ἔσφηλεν δ' ἐπὶ δίνην
…ἔσφηλεν δ' ἐπὶ δίνην. αἱ δ' ἐπὶ μακρὸν ἄϋσαν… "She [the amphipolos] threw it into the eddy. They cried out loudly…" Same girl, same scene, same Greek word.
Robert Fitzgerald1961

…they ran and passed a ball to a rhythmic beat,

when the king's daughter threw her ball off line

and missed, and put it in the whirling stream…

Robert Fagles1996

…struck up a game of ball.

The ball — the princess suddenly tossed it to a maid

but it missed the girl, it splashed in a deep swirling pool…

Daniel Mendelsohn2025

…by now she had yoked the mules…

Just then the princess threw the ball to one of her maids —

But it missed the maid and plunged into the eddying depths.

Emily Wilson2017

…they ran and passed a ball to a rhythmic beat,

The princess threw the ball towards a slave girl,

who missed, and it fell in the swirling river.

Same girl, same scene, same Greek word. Three translators reach for the neutral "maid" or sidestep the noun entirely. Emily Wilson alone calls a free-born Phaeacian royal companion a "slave girl."

Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58
▸ "Slaves with braided hair": Nausicaa addresses her friends Od. 6.199 · ἀμφιπόλοισιν καλλικόμοισιν
αὐτίκα δ' ἀμφιπόλοισιν ἐκέκλετο καλλικόμοισιν "Straightaway she called out to her fair-haired amphipoloi." The ornamental, affectionate epithet: these are her companions, not subordinates.
Robert Fitzgerald1961

Turning, she called out to her maids-in-waiting:

"Stay with me! Does the sight of a man scare you?"

Robert Fagles1996

She called out to her girls with lovely braids:

"Stop, my friends! Why run when you see a man?"

Daniel Mendelsohn2025

And now she commanded her maids, those women with beautiful braids:

"Stay right where you are, girls! One look at a man and you're fleeing?"

Emily Wilson2017

And then she called her slaves with braided hair.

"Wait, girls! Why are you running from this man?"

The companions Nausicaa calls "my friends" (Robert Fagles), or "girls" warmly (Robert Fitzgerald, Daniel Mendelsohn), are Emily Wilson's "slaves with braided hair." The ornamental Homeric epithet καλλικόμοισιν (affectionate, formulaic, free of status content) is grafted onto a slave label the Greek does not bear here.

Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58
▸ Canapés: Emily Wilson's most-quoted anachronism, plus a "humble slave" housekeeper Od. 4.55 · εἴδατα · ταμίη
ταμίη δ' ἐπέθηκεν ἄρ' εἴδατα πολλά ταμίη = housekeeper with authority over storerooms (a position of trust). εἴδατα = "foods, delicacies" (generic).
Robert Fitzgerald1961

The grave housekeeper brought her tray of loaves

with many savories to lavish on them…

Robert Fagles1996

A staid housekeeper brought on bread to serve them,

appetizers aplenty too, lavish with her bounty.

Daniel Mendelsohn2025

Then the housekeeper, worthy woman, brought in the bread and served it,

Glad to have so many dishes to set out from her storerooms inside.

Emily Wilson2017

and a humble slave girl brought bread and many canapés,

a lavish spread.

canapés is a French loan-word coined in the 18th century for cocktail-party food. It is class-inflected in a way the Greek εἴδατα ("foods, delicacies") is not. The compounding error is the housekeeper: ταμίη denotes administrative authority over the storerooms, so Emily Wilson's humble slave girl both mislabels her status and adds a deprecating adjective absent from Homer.

Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58 · Richard Whitaker · Acta Classica 2020

One English word ("slave") for a dozen Greek roles

Greek termStandard meaningEmily Wilson rendersThe internal contradiction
ἀμφίπολος female attendant / companion (status unspecified) slave / house girl Used of free Phaeacian women in Nausicaa's retinue. See all four passages above.
θεράπων attendant, squire ("dependent but free") slave Includes elite warriors and the squires of named heroes; cannot be slaves.
κήρυξ herald (elite, ritually protected official) slave (usually) At Od. 2.38, where the herald handles the sceptre, Emily Wilson silently switches to "the competent official." Eurybates (19.244), a Black African herald, she calls "valet."
ταμίη housekeeper (administrative authority over storerooms) slave girl (often "humble slave girl") The male equivalent ταμίης (Aeolus, Od. 10.21) she translates "steward of the winds," because Aeolus is a god.
ποιμήν shepherd slave (4.86) The equivalent at 13.222 she calls "shepherd," because that shepherd is Athena in disguise.
δμωαί enslaved household women (actual slaves) girls (passim, esp. Od. 22) The only group Emily Wilson should call slaves she calls "girls." See the execution passage above.

Richard Whitaker, citing William G. Thalmann (2011): Homer "tends to avoid words unambiguously meaning 'slave'," a feature scholars read as Homer's own indirect mitigation of the institution. "Because it obscures this significant feature of the epic, Emily Wilson's translation, ironically, could not be used for research into 'slavery in Homer's Odyssey.'"

03.

And when they really are slaves, why does she call them "girls"?

Eight times in forty lines, at the moment of these women's execution, Emily Wilson softens "women" to "girls," a register she doesn't reach for anywhere else in the poem.

Od. 22.421–473 δμωαί δμωαί = "enslaved women of the household." This is the rare place in Homer where the word unambiguously means slaves: the twelve household women who slept with the suitors are forced to clean up the carnage, then hanged. Every other major translator calls them "women." Emily Wilson calls them "girls."
Robert Fitzgerald1961
Near-universal praise

The women, herded together, came in tears…

there Telemachus, with a deft seaman's hand,

caught them in a noose-line that strung them up by the necks…

[Robert Fitzgerald uses "women" throughout the execution, never "girls"]

Robert Fagles1996
Most widely taught

The women crowded in, huddling all together…

so the women's heads were trapped in a line,

nooses yanking their necks up, one by one…

Daniel Mendelsohn2025
Early strong reception

…they brought the female slaves out of the strong-built Hall…

"Let the death that I inflict on these women not be a clean one!"

So did the women hold their heads all in a row…

Emily Wilson2017
Landmark; deeply contested

Twelve stepped away from honor: those twelve girls

"The girls must help. Then clean my stately chairs…"

Sobbing desperately the girls came, weeping…

just so the girls, their heads all in a row,

were strung up with the noose around their necks…

This is the inverse of §02. Where Emily Wilson imposed slave status on free women, here she removes adult status from women who actually are enslaved. δμωαί (a word that does mean "enslaved women") becomes "girls," even as the same passage also (correctly) uses "fifty female slaves." The inconsistency is internal to Emily Wilson's own translation.

Note what doesn't happen elsewhere: Emily Wilson does not call Nausicaa, or Penelope, or Helen "girls." The diminutive arrives only when adult enslaved women are about to be executed, at the precise moment the Greek confronts the reader most directly with what slavery meant. The softening is not a quirk of vocabulary: it is selective, and the selection lands at the worst possible point.

Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58 · Richard Whitaker · Acta Classica 2020, p. 248
04.

Why is Emily Wilson casting doubt on our hero? From the very first line..

The literal opening line of the poem. The very first word about Odysseus, and Emily Wilson renders it "complicated."

Od. 1.1 πολύτροπος "Of many turns / ways": cunning and much-travelled simultaneously. The poem's first word about Odysseus. Every translator's calling card.
Robert Fitzgerald1961
Near-universal praise

Sing in me, Muse, and through me tell the story

of that man skilled in all ways of contending,

the wanderer, harried for years on end,

after he plundered the stronghold

on the proud height of Troy.

Robert Fagles1996
Most widely taught

Sing to me of the man, Muse, the man of twists and turns

driven time and again off course, once he had plundered

the hallowed heights of Troy.

Many cities of men he saw and learned their minds,

many pains he suffered, heartsick on the open sea…

Daniel Mendelsohn2025
Early strong reception

Tell me the tale of a man, Muse, who had so many roundabout ways

To wander, driven off course, after sacking Troy's hallowed keep;

Many the peoples whose cities he saw and whose ways of thinking he learned,

Many the toils he suffered at sea, anguish in his heart…

Emily Wilson2017
Landmark; deeply contested

Tell me about a complicated man.

Muse, tell me how he wandered and was lost

when he had wrecked the holy town of Troy,

and where he went, and who he met, the pain

he suffered in the storms at sea…

complicated is a single psychological adjective that forecloses the Greek's deliberate spatial / tactical / moral ambiguity. πολύτροπος means "of many turns": cunning and much-travelled simultaneously. Corinne Pache (whose overall verdict on Emily Wilson is positive) singles out this opening as the moment the translation becomes "sturdier than the original: a bit less open-ended, less polytropic, less complicated."

Corinne Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58 · Richard Whitaker · Acta Classica 2020, pp. 245–47

The wider pattern: five more receipts

  1. From the stock epithet πολύμητις ("resourceful," "of many wiles"), at Od. 9.1 Emily Wilson extracts "Wily Odysseus, the lord of lies"; and at 24.303, "lying Odysseus." Richard Whitaker: "she warns the reader against believing anything that may follow."
  2. From πολύτλας ("much-enduring") she derives "hardened" (23.110) and "ruthless" (24.349).
  3. The suitors' ὑπέρβιον ὕβριν ("violent insolence," per Anthony Verity; "outrageous and arrogant," per Peter Green) becomes Emily Wilson's mild "ungentlemanly suitors" (16.410). Her summary calls them men who simply "behaved badly" (p. 551) where the Greek talks of men who "perpetrated a monstrous wrong."
  4. The Cyclops is described in Od. 9.214–15 as "a savage with no understanding of either justice or laws" (Anthony Verity). Emily Wilson translates: "a man of courage, wild, and lacking knowledge of the normal customs." She eschews "savage" because the word carries "a legacy of colonialism … anachronistic in the world of The Odyssey" (Emily Wilson, p. 88).
  5. Odysseus's stock action verbs are uniformly hardened: ὀλέσας (universally translated "having lost [all his companions]") becomes in Emily Wilson "having caused the death of all your men" (9.534), "having destroyed your men" (11.117), "let his loyal men be killed" (19.272). Richard Whitaker: "But why, one has to ask, would Odysseus, who is the narrator in all three places, inculpate himself in this way?"
05.

Bronze-Age epic, or Netflix show?

"Babysit." "Tote bag." "Cushy." "Snuggling." "Whooshed." Welcome to Ithaca. And three lines later, "vernal Dawn."

Richard Whitaker accepts that Emily Wilson sometimes uses higher-register diction effectively. The problem is the simultaneous deployment of colloquialism and elevated archaism within the same text, what he calls "two levels collid[ing] jarringly." It is not that "tote bag" is a bad word. It is that "tote bag" sits four chapters away from "vernal Dawn," and the reader is never sure which Ithaca they are reading about.

Emily Wilson, contemporary register

"You must be joking" 1.64 babysit 7.12 whooshed 10.55 snuggling 10.218 cutest 10.278 Teenagers 11.38 she glugs it down 12.106 tote bag 13.438 cushy 14.47 make nice 14.387 standoffishness 15.72 games of darts 17.168 cottoned on 18.95 pace themselves 21.295 fluffy pillows 24.257

Emily Wilson, elevated register (same translation)

vernal Dawn 5.228 seven days and ten 5.277 fleet-foot deer 6.104 wreathed about by boundless sea 10.195 with dangers compassed round 17.526 "Mentor, for shame!" 2.242

Richard Whitaker calls out one line where the two levels collide directly: "mighty Zeus, the god of home owners" (22.335). He also demonstrates that one of Emily Wilson's "lines" of verse is in fact a verbatim sentence from E.V. Rieu's (1946) prose translation, broken into pentameters. "Emily Wilson's vocabulary and style (for the most part plain and flat, often prosaic) remind me of nothing so much as E.V. Rieu's relentlessly 1940s-contemporary, ordinary-language prose translation."
