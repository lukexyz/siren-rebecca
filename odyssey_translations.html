CHAPMAN
1616

George Chapman

Nathaniel Butter, London, 1616
Historical Iambic pentameter Keats's George Chapman (1616)
The translation immortalised by Keats's sonnet "On First Looking into George Chapman's (1616) Homer." George Chapman (1616) did not read Greek fluently and worked partly from Spondanus's Greek-Latin edition and Andreas Divus's Latin translation. His interpretive framework reads Odysseus as a Stoic moral hero — "the mind's inward, constant and unconquerd Empire." Modern academic treatment is literary-historical: the question is George Chapman's (1616) poetics and Elizabethan reception of Homer, not fidelity to the Greek.
Colin Burrow
All Souls College, Oxford
LRB · Vol 24 No 12 · 2002
◆ ILIAD GREAT; ODYSSEY WEAK
Strengths
  • Iliad praised for "elemental" mastery — fire, violence, transformation
  • Defends George Chapman (1616) against Arnold's dismissal of "Elizabethan fancifulness"
  • Superior to "timid, reverent and excessively archaeological" modern translations
Weaknesses
  • Odyssey's second half "translated in some haste, and with little sympathy, probably 1611–15"
  • Allegorical Stoic lens causes George Chapman (1616) to lose interest when Odysseus is wily or mendacious
  • Result: mechanical execution in the Odyssey's latter books
"Not quite as great as Keats says he is."— Colin Burrow, London Review of Books (2002)

▶ 1725 Alexander Pope Bernard Lintot Heroic couplets
Arnold's critique endures
 1
POPE
1725

Alexander Pope

Bernard Lintot, London, 1725–26 (5 vols.) · Co-translated with Broome & Fenton
Historical Heroic couplets Literary monument
A literary monument rather than a scholarly translation. Alexander Pope's (1725) Odyssey is now studied as 18th-century English poetry, not as access to Homer. Matthew Arnold's 1861 lecture "On Translating Homer" attacked Alexander Pope (1725) directly and defined the terms of translation criticism for the following century.
Matthew Arnold
Professor of Poetry, Oxford
On Translating Homer · 1861
▼ FOUNDATIONAL CRITIQUE
  • Alexander Pope's (1725) "nobleness" is not Homer's — Alexander Pope (1725) is "literary" where Homer is "plain"
  • Ornate couplets betray Homer's speed and directness
  • Four essential Homeric qualities (rapid, plain, direct, noble) are all violated
"It is not so much a translation of Homer as a transformation of him into something un-Homeric."— Matthew Arnold, On Translating Homer (1861)

Arnold's lecture became the standard frame for all subsequent Odyssey translation criticism. Its four criteria — speed, plainness, directness, nobility — are still invoked by reviewers of Emily Wilson (2017), Daniel Mendelsohn (2025), and Peter Green (2018).

▶ 1946 E. V. Rieu Penguin Classics Plain prose / novelistic
Popular; classicists sceptical
 2
RIEU
1946

E. V. Rieu

Penguin Classics No. 1, 1946 · Revised by D. C. H. Rieu, 2003
3 million+ copies Plain prose Founded Penguin Classics
The first Penguin Classic, and the translation that made Homer genuinely accessible to a mass audience. E.V. Rieu (1946) explicitly wanted to render Homer "as a novel," rejecting scholar-to-scholar archaism. Sold over 3 million copies. Classicists, however, have criticised its systematic embellishment and the suppression of the poem's oral, formulaic character. OUP eventually replaced it with Walter Shewring (1980) and then Anthony Verity (2016).
Isabelle Vanderschelden
Translation studies scholar
The Translator · Vol 20 No 2 · 2014
◆ HISTORICALLY SIGNIFICANT; METHODOLOGICALLY FLAWED
  • Egalitarian, post-WWII democratisation of Homer was culturally important
  • Made the poem genuinely readable for a mass audience for the first time
  • Dynamic equivalence: regularly embroidered Homer's verse rather than translating it
  • Novelistic framing distorts the poem's oral, formulaic, repetitive character
"In the past there has been too much translation by scholars for scholars, resulting in a weird kind of Greek-English."— E. V. Rieu, quoted in Vanderschelden (2014)

FITZGERALD
1961

Robert Fitzgerald

Doubleday, 1961 · Farrar, Straus & Giroux (in print)
Bollingen Prize Flexible blank verse Standard for three generations
Considered by many scholars the finest poetic rendering of the Odyssey in English. Robert Fitzgerald's (1961) flexible blank verse, influenced by Ezra Pound, achieves a register that is simultaneously elevated and natural. Winner of the Bollingen Prize. Standard classroom text for three generations. Steiner's 1961 Kenyon Review review placed it in the lineage of George Chapman (1616) and Alexander Pope (1725); that assessment has largely held.

Whitaker's case against Emily Wilson (2017) — "distorting and misrepresenting what the Greek text says"

Richard Whitaker (UCT) makes three sustained charges against Emily Wilson's (2017) Odyssey: that she recasts Odysseus as a villain by editing the Greek; that she imposes a uniform "slave" label across a dozen distinct social roles ("Pan-Slaveism"); and that her diction is tonally incoherent — "babysit" and "whooshed" alongside "vernal Dawn." The passages below diff Emily Wilson (2017) against the three other translations in the local ref/ folder. Yellow highlights mark Emily Wilson's (2017) contested rendering; green highlights mark the more accurate alternatives Whitaker (and Pache) defend.

I
Character Distortions
Emily Wilson (2017) reframes Odysseus as villain through altered epithets and loaded word choices
1 passage
Od. 1.1 πολύτροπος — "complicated" vs the Greek's ambiguity The poem's first word about Odysseus. Every translator's calling card.
ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, πολύτροπον, ὃς μάλα πολλὰ / πλάγχθη… "Tell me, Muse, of the polytropos man, who was driven far journeys…" · πολύτροπος = "of many turns/ways" — cunning AND much-travelled simultaneously.
Robert Fagles1996

Sing to me of the man, Muse, the man of twists and turns

driven time and again off course, once he had plundered

the hallowed heights of Troy.

Many cities of men he saw and learned their minds,

many pains he suffered, heartsick on the open sea…

Robert Fitzgerald1961

Sing in me, Muse, and through me tell the story

of that man skilled in all ways of contending,

the wanderer, harried for years on end,

after he plundered the stronghold

on the proud height of Troy.

Daniel Mendelsohn2025

Tell me the tale of a man, Muse, who had so many roundabout ways

To wander, driven off course, after sacking Troy's hallowed keep;

Many the peoples whose cities he saw and whose ways of thinking he learned,

Many the toils he suffered at sea, anguish in his heart…

Emily Wilson2017

Tell me about a complicated man.

Muse, tell me how he wandered and was lost

when he had wrecked the holy town of Troy,

and where he went, and who he met, the pain

he suffered in the storms at sea…

Pache · BMCR 2018.10.58

"complicated" is a single psychological adjective that forecloses the Greek's deliberate spatial/tactical/moral ambiguity. πολύτροπος means "of many turns/ways" — cunning and much-travelled simultaneously. Every other translator preserves at least the ambiguity of direction/skill. Emily Wilson (2017) imports a modern register of interior complexity absent from the Greek.

Pache's overall verdict on Emily Wilson (2017) remained positive — but she singles out this opening as the moment the translation becomes "sturdier than the original: a bit less open-ended, less polytropic, less complicated."

II
Textual Misrepresentation
Emily Wilson (2017) omits or suppresses phrases that exonerate Odysseus and name the men's moral fault
1 passage
Od. 1.5–9 ἀτασθαλίῃσιν · νήπιοι · ἱέμενός περ — Whitaker's principal exhibit Emily Wilson (2017) omits "though he longed to" + the entire line about the men's "blind recklessness."
ἀλλ' οὐδ' ὧς ἑτάρους ἐρρύσατο, ἱέμενός περ / αὐτῶν γὰρ σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὄλοντο, νήπιοι "Yet not even so could he save his companions, though he longed to; for through their own reckless folly they perished — children, fools." The line tells us Odysseus tried; his men died from a named moral failing.
Robert Fagles1996

many pains he suffered, heartsick on the open sea,

fighting to save his life and bring his comrades home.

But he could not save them from disaster, hard as he strove —

the recklessness of their own ways destroyed them all,

the blind fools, they devoured the cattle of the Sun

and the Sungod wiped from sight the day of their return.

Launch out on his story, Muse…

Robert Fitzgerald1961

in his deep heart at sea, while he fought only

to save his life, to bring his shipmates home.

But not by will nor valor could he save them,

for their own recklessness destroyed them all —

children and fools, they killed and feasted on

the cattle of Lord Hēlios, the Sun,

and he who moves all day through heaven

took from their eyes the dawn of their return.

Daniel Mendelsohn2025

Many the toils he suffered at sea, anguish in his heart

As he struggled to safeguard his life and the homecoming of his companions.

But he did not save his companions even so, though he longed to,

For their heedlessness destroyed them, theirs and nobody else's —

Fools that they were, like children, who devoured the sun-god Hyperion's

Cattle, and so he took from them the day of their homecoming.

Goddess, start where you will…

Emily Wilson2017

…the pain he suffered in the storms at sea, and how

he worked to save his life and bring his men

back home.

He failed to keep them safe; poor fools,

they ate the Sun God's cattle, and the god

kept them from home.

Now goddess, child of Zeus,

tell the old story for our modern times.

Whitaker · Acta Classica 2020, p. 246

Where the Greek says ἀλλ' οὐδ' ὧς ("yet not even so" — despite his efforts) Odysseus could not save his men, "Emily Wilson (2017) leaves out this important qualification. And then, with breathtaking insouciance, she simply omits to translate the key phrase 'though he longed to' (ἱέμενός περ, Od. 1.6) as well as, in its entirety, the crucial line: 'it was through their own blind recklessness that they perished' (Od. 1.7)."

"Finally, where the Greek says the sun god actively 'took away' (ἀφείλετο, 1.9) their homecoming, i.e. destroyed the men because of their recklessness, Emily Wilson (2017) obscures this with her misleading 'kept them from home.' Without it, Odysseus's failure looks like indifference rather than tragic inadequacy, and the men die for no named reason."

Note the alignment between Robert Fitzgerald (1961), Robert Fagles (1996), and Daniel Mendelsohn (2025) — three very different stylists all preserving the same two semantic loads (Odysseus's effort + the men's named moral fault) that Emily Wilson (2017) alone removes.

III
"Pan-Slaveism" Policy
Emily Wilson (2017) applies "slave" to free attendants (ἀμφίπολος, θεράπων, κήρυξ, ταμίη) — and withholds it from women who actually are enslaved
5 passages
Od. 6.18–21 ἀμφίπολοι — Nausicaa's attendants become "slaves" Free Phaeacian women at a royal court rendered as enslaved persons.
τῇ δὲ δύω ἀμφίπολοι Χαρίτεσσιν εἰκυῖαι / σταθμοῖιν ἑκάτερθε κάθευδον… "Two amphipoloi like the Graces were sleeping, one on each side of the doorposts." ἀμφίπολος = "female attendant or companion" — status unspecified or implicitly free in a royal context. Nausicaa's ἀμφίπολοι are daughters of free Phaeacian citizens.
Robert Fagles1996

She made her way to the gaily painted room

where a young girl lay asleep…

a match for the deathless gods in build and beauty,

Nausicaa, the daughter of generous King Alcinous.

Two handmaids fair as the Graces slept beside her,

Emily Wilson's (2017) "Pan-Slaveism" policy (her own description: she wants "to mark the fact that the idealized society depicted in the Odyssey is one where slavery is shockingly taken for granted") leads her to translate ἀμφίπολος as "slave" — even when the women in question are free Phaeacian companions to a royal princess.

Whitaker: "Emily Wilson (2017) is simply wrong when she uses 'slave' (as she often does) to render θεράπων ('attendant' or 'squire') and κήρυξ ('herald'). The category buckles under its own internal contradictions.

The companions Nausicaa calls "my friends" (in Robert Fagles (1996)), or simply "girls" addressed warmly (Robert Fitzgerald (1961), Daniel Mendelsohn (2025)), are Emily Wilson's (2017) "slaves with braided hair." The ornamental Homeric epithet καλλικόμοισιν — affectionate, formulaic, free of status content — is grafted onto a slave label the Greek does not bear here.

canapés is a French loan-word coined in the 18th century for a specific kind of cocktail-party food. It is class-inflected in a way the Greek εἴδατα ("foods, delicacies") is not. Robert Fagles's (1996) "appetizers aplenty" and Daniel Mendelsohn's (2025) "so many dishes" keep the term generic; Robert Fitzgerald's (1961) "savories" stays in register.

The compounding error is the housekeeper. ταμίη denotes administrative authority over the storerooms — Robert Fagles's (1996) "staid housekeeper," Robert Fitzgerald's (1961) "grave housekeeper," Daniel Mendelsohn's (2025) "housekeeper, worthy woman" all preserve her dignified role. Emily Wilson's (2017) humble slave girl both mislabels her status and adds a deprecating adjective absent from Homer.

This is the inverse of the Book 6 issue. Where Emily Wilson (2017) imposed slave status on free women, here she removes adult status from women who are actually enslaved. δμωαί — a word that does mean "enslaved women" — is rendered "girls" while the same passage also (correctly) uses "fifty female slaves." The inconsistency is internal to Emily Wilson's (2017) own translation.

The consequences are real: at the moment Homer's text confronts the reader most directly with adult enslaved women being killed, Emily Wilson's (2017) lexicon softens "women" to "girls" eight times in roughly forty lines. Daniel Mendelsohn's (2025) "female slaves" and "women," Robert Fagles's (1996) "women" throughout, and Robert Fitzgerald's (1961) "women" all keep the moral weight at adult register.

This is the rare passage where Whitaker's overall verdict on Emily Wilson (2017) softens. He quotes all three side by side and observes that Peter Green (2018) and Anthony Verity's (2016) "long, six-beat line allows them to translate almost every word of the Greek original. But it also, sometimes, leads to a wordiness, a certain turgidity in their versions that makes one sigh for the concision of Emily Wilson (2017)."

Stallings, in the TLS, joined those defending the choice. The argument is whether "there will be blood" recaptures the prophetic force or imports a contemporary register that pulls the reader out of the archaic world. The four columns above let the reader decide.

Whitaker's further charges — character distortion, Pan-Slaveism, tonal incoherence, errata

Character distortions: Odysseus as villain, suitors and Cyclops as victims

Whitaker argues Emily Wilson (2017) pursues "a specific twenty-first-century agenda: casting Odysseus in a reprehensible, villainous light (as a liar, pirate, and colonizer) while overly sympathizing with his adversaries." She does this not by interpretive paratext but by editing the Greek itself:

  • From the stock epithet πολύμητις ("resourceful," "of many wiles"), at Od. 9.1 she extracts "Wily Odysseus, the lord of lies" — and at 24.303, "lying Odysseus." Whitaker: "she warns the reader against believing anything that may follow."
  • From πολύτλας ("much-enduring") she derives "hardened" (23.110) and "ruthless" (24.349).
  • The suitors' ὑπέρβιον ὕβριν ("violent insolence" — Anthony Verity (2016); "outrageous and arrogant" — Peter Green (2018)) becomes Emily Wilson's (2017) mild "ungentlemanly suitors" (16.410). Her summary calls them men who simply "behaved badly" (p. 551) where the Greek talks of men who "perpetrated a monstrous wrong."
  • The Cyclops is described in Od. 9.214–15 as "a savage with no understanding of either justice or laws" (Anthony Verity (2016)). Emily Wilson (2017) translates: "a man of courage, wild, and lacking knowledge of the normal customs." She eschews "savage" because the word carries "a legacy of colonialism … anachronistic in the world of The Odyssey" (Emily Wilson (2017), p. 88).
  • Odysseus's stock action verbs are uniformly hardened: ὀλέσας (universally translated "having lost [all his companions]") becomes in Emily Wilson (2017) "having caused the death of all your men" (9.534–35), "having destroyed your men" (11.117), "let his loyal men be killed" (19.272–73). Whitaker: "But why, one has to ask, would Odysseus, who is the narrator in all three places, inculpate himself in this way?"
Whitaker, Acta Classica 2020, pp. 245–47

"Pan-Slaveism": one English word for a dozen Greek roles

Emily Wilson's (2017) own justification (Norton ed., pp. 88–89): "the need to acknowledge the fact and the horror of slavery … seems to me to outweigh the need to specify, in every instance, the type of slave." Whitaker's response: the policy fails because some of the Greek words she labels "slave" do not mean slave at all. The errors are not occasional — they are systematic and visible the moment the role's social function contradicts the slave label.

ἀμφίπολος female attendant / companion (status unspecified) slave / house girl Used of free Phaeacian women in Nausicaa's retinue — see passages 3–5 above.
θεράπων attendant, squire — "dependent but free" (Raaflaub 2011) slave Includes elite warriors and the squires of named heroes; cannot be slaves.
κήρυξ herald — elite, ritually protected official slave — usually At Od. 2.38, where the herald handles the sceptre in the assembly, Emily Wilson (2017) silently switches to "the competent official." Eurybates (19.244–47), a Black African herald, she calls "valet." Whitaker: a single hapax used to dodge the difficulty.
ταμίη housekeeper — administrative authority over storerooms slave girl (often "humble slave girl") The male equivalent ταμίης (Aeolus, Od. 10.21) she translates "steward of the winds" — because Aeolus is a god.
ποιμήν shepherd slave (4.86) The equivalent ἐπιβώτορι μήλων at 13.222 she calls "shepherd" — because that shepherd is Athena in disguise.

"Because it obscures this significant feature of the epic, Emily Wilson's (2017) translation, ironically, could not be used for research into 'slavery in Homer's Odyssey.'"

Whitaker, Acta Classica 2020, pp. 248–49

Tonal incoherence: "babysit" alongside "vernal Dawn"

Whitaker accepts that Emily Wilson (2017) sometimes uses higher-register diction effectively. The problem is the simultaneous deployment of colloquialism and elevated archaism within the same text — what he calls a clash of "two levels collid[ing] jarringly."

Colloquial diction (Emily Wilson's (2017), in narrative as well as speech): "You must be joking" (1.64); "babysit" (7.12); "whooshed" (10.55); "snuggling" (10.218); "cutest" (10.278); "Teenagers" (11.38); "she glugs it down" (12.106); "tote bag" (13.438); "cushy" (14.47); "make nice" (14.387); "standoffishness" (15.72); "games of darts" (17.168); "cottoned on" (18.95); "pace themselves" (21.295, of drinking wine); "fluffy pillows" (24.257).

Elevated diction, in the same translation: "vernal Dawn" (5.228); "seven days and ten" (5.277); "fleet-foot deer" (6.104); "wreathed about by boundless sea" (10.195); "with dangers compassed round" (17.526); "Mentor, for shame!" (2.242).

Whitaker calls out one line where the two levels collide: "mighty Zeus, the god of home owners" (22.335) — and notes the difficulty of producing iambic pentameter that is recognisably poetry in the absence of rhyme. He demonstrates that one of Emily Wilson's (2017) "lines" of verse is in fact a verbatim sentence from E.V. Rieu's (1946) prose translation, broken into pentameters. "Emily Wilson's (2017) vocabulary and style — for the most part plain and flat, often prosaic — remind me of nothing so much as E.V. Rieu's (1946) relentlessly 1940s-contemporary, ordinary-language prose translation."

Whitaker, Acta Classica 2020, pp. 249–51
